About the plant

An evergreen shrub with slender, lance-shaped leaves arranged in whorls of three, oleander produces clusters of five-petalled flowers in white, pink, or deep rose through the warm months. In the wild it lines dry riverbeds and rocky coastal ground across the Mediterranean basin, where its deep roots and waxy leaves help it withstand long stretches without rain. Nearly every tissue in the plant carries potent cardiac glycosides, a defence so effective that livestock and most insects leave it untouched — one reason it survives so readily along roadsides and neglected slopes.

Everyday uses

  • OrnamentalWidely planted along medians and boundary hedges for its fast growth and long bloom.
  • Historical medicineExtracts were once studied for heart conditions, but the margin between dose and poison is razor-thin — modern use is strictly pharmaceutical.
  • Cultural importanceA fixture of Mediterranean courtyard gardens for centuries, and a recurring motif in regional art and poetry.
  • Erosion controlDeep, spreading roots stabilise dry slopes and highway embankments.
  • Wildlife interactionsToxicity keeps grazing animals away, while moths and bees still visit the flowers for nectar.

Fun facts

Its toxins are so concentrated that a handful of leaves is considered enough to seriously harm a child or a small animal.
Oleander appears in wall paintings from Pompeii, suggesting it was already a prized ornamental nearly two thousand years ago.
Its drought tolerance and disregard from grazing animals make it a favourite for highway medians and neglected public plantings worldwide.
Burning any part of the plant can release irritant compounds, which is why gardeners are advised never to add clippings to a bonfire.
